Understanding Disposition Dimensions

Read this before exploring your profile. It will help you make sense of what you see.

What are disposition dimensions?

As teachers, we carry deeply held dispositions about what learning is, what relationships with students should look like, and what our role is in the classroom. Most of the time, we don't examine these dispositions — they just shape what we do.

This survey maps your dispositions across 5 dimensions. Each dimension is a tension between two orientations — neither is right or wrong. They are rooted in different but legitimate educational traditions and values.

The goal is not to judge where you are, but to help you see clearly — and to reflect on whether your dispositions and your practices are aligned.

How to read your profile

Each point on the profile is one survey question. The further out it sits, the more strongly you agreed with that statement. Q1–Q5 (amber) are traditional dispositions; Q6–Q10 (teal) are progressive. Opposite points are paired dimensions — they represent the two poles of the same tension.

A large amber shape with a small teal shape suggests a more traditional orientation. A large teal shape with a small amber shape suggests a more progressive one. A profile that is large on both sides suggests you hold both dispositions actively — which is often the most complex position.

The 5 Dimensions

Q1
It is essential for a teacher to keep social distance from students to maintain discipline.
Q6
A teacher should be like a friend to their students.
Distance pole

Maintaining professional boundaries. Authority is preserved through distance -- being too close can undermine discipline and respect.

Formal address, separate physical space, limited personal sharing, clear role boundaries.

Friendship pole

Warmth and closeness are the foundation of learning. Relationships precede instruction -- students learn from people they feel safe with.

Informal interactions, personal sharing, high approachability, students see teacher as an ally.

Holding both

Research on authoritative teaching (Baumrind, 1971) shows the most effective teachers are warm and structured. Holding both means being deeply relational while maintaining clarity about your role.

What it means to shift

Moving toward friendship requires vulnerability. Moving toward distance can feel like protecting your authority. Shifts here often follow significant relationships -- a student who trusted you, or a moment where distance clearly failed.

For students

Adolescents are highly sensitive to whether a teacher likes them. A strongly distant teacher may be perceived as cold. A strongly friendly teacher without structure can create confusion about expectations.

Q2
Students who are not willing to open up cannot develop a supportive relationship with the teacher.
Q7
Teachers should create opportunities for less open students to open up.
Fixed pole

Openness is the student's responsibility. If a student does not engage, that is on them -- the teacher's role is to teach, not to coax participation.

Uniform expectations, limited differentiation for shy students, leaving it to students to engage.

Adaptive pole

The teacher actively reads students and creates conditions for each one to open up. They adjust their approach -- more patient with one, more direct with another.

Differentiated engagement, noticing withdrawn students, creating entry points for quiet learners.

Holding both

Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development -- meeting students where they are while holding expectations. Holding both means you believe students carry responsibility AND you build the scaffolding that makes engagement possible for each student.

What it means to shift

Shifting toward adaptive often happens when a teacher encounters a student who clearly wants to engage but cannot. Shifting back can come from burnout or when efforts are not being reciprocated.

For students

Introverted, anxious, or marginalised students suffer most under a fixed approach. They often want to engage but need the teacher to create the bridge. Without it, they disengage silently.

Q3
A student must follow discipline and behave properly before a teacher can make them learn.
Q8
By understanding a student's emotional struggles, a teacher creates the necessary safe space to learn.
Discipline pole

Learning requires order. A student must be regulated before they can learn. Behaviour management comes first -- establishing compliance before instruction.

Strict behaviour protocols, consequences before conversations, academic content conditional on conduct.

Relationship pole

Behaviour is communication. A student who is acting out is telling you something about their internal state. Understanding that state creates conditions for learning.

Curiosity before consequence, asking what is going on, seeing behaviour as data.

Holding both

Trauma-informed pedagogy (Brunzell et al., 2016) and restorative practices argue that safety is the precondition for learning. Holding both means: clear structures AND seeing every behaviour as worth understanding. Structure provides safety; relationship provides the motivation to use it.

What it means to shift

Shifting toward relationship often happens after a breakthrough moment -- when curiosity worked where consequences had failed. Shifting toward discipline often follows relational fatigue or when inconsistency has created chaos.

For students

Students with trauma histories, attachment difficulties, or neurodivergence are most affected here. For them, behaviour IS the message -- and a purely discipline-first response can deepen mistrust. Classrooms without structure also create anxiety.

Q4
To focus on academics, it is important to leave emotional & social issues outside the classroom.
Q9
Classroom is a social space where emotional and social issues are natural to come up.
Academic pole

The classroom is for learning content. Emotional and social issues belong elsewhere. Bringing them in distracts from the academic purpose.

Deflecting personal disclosures, keeping to the lesson, referring emotional issues to specialists.

Holistic pole

Learning is inseparable from the whole child. A student who is grieving or anxious cannot simply set that aside. The classroom must hold human experience as part of academics, not instead of them.

Acknowledging students lives, building SEL into lessons, making space for feelings before content.

Holding both

Bronfenbrenner's ecological model and SEL research (Durlak et al., 2011) show social-emotional learning enhances academic outcomes. Holding both means teaching content rigorously AND creating a classroom climate where the whole child is acknowledged.

What it means to shift

Shifting toward holistic often follows a student clearly needing to be seen before they could engage academically. Shifting toward academic can come from pressure: curriculum targets, exam performance, time constraints.

For students

Students going through difficulty at home often need one teacher to acknowledge what they are carrying. A wholly academic environment can feel dehumanising. However, students also need teachers who hold the space for learning.

Q5
Teachers should avoid making mistakes in front of students as it undermines their authority.
Q10
Developing a safe space to make errors and seek feedback is important for continuous learning.
Perfect pole

The teacher must model competence and certainty. Mistakes in front of students undermine authority and trust. The teacher should appear to always know the answer.

Avoiding admitting uncertainty, deflecting when wrong, high stakes around being seen to fail.

Growth pole

The teacher models learning openly -- including mistakes, confusion, and revision of thinking. Showing how you navigate errors teaches students more than showing a finished product.

Saying I do not know -- let us find out together, celebrating wrong turns, sharing the learning process.

Holding both

Dweck's growth mindset research shows the teacher who models fallibility while demonstrating mastery creates the most powerful learning environment. Holding both means you bring deep expertise AND let students see you struggle and learn.

What it means to shift

Shifting toward growth requires psychological safety and accepting that authority is not diminished by uncertainty. Shifting toward perfect can come from cultural pressure, accountability systems, or past experiences of humiliation. This dimension is often the most personal.

For students

When a teacher never shows mistakes, students learn that competence means never being wrong -- creating perfectionism and fear of failure. Teachers who model growth give students explicit permission to struggle and learn from failure.

Why this matters for students: When teachers on the same team hold very different dispositions about learning and relationships, students experience inconsistency — different expectations, different emotional climates, different standards. Understanding your team's spread helps you work more intentionally as a collective.
